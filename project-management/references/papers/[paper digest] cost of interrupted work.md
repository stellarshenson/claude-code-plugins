**The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress (2008)**

A controlled laboratory experiment on whether the *context* of an interruption changes its
disruption cost. Forty-eight subjects played a human-resources manager answering email in a
simulated office, under three within-subject conditions: no interruption, an interruption on the
same topic as the task, and an interruption on an unrelated topic. The expected result was that
same-context interruptions would be cheaper than different-context ones. Neither held. Context made
no difference, and interrupted work was completed *faster* than uninterrupted work, not slower. The
cost showed up somewhere else: subjects reported significantly higher stress, frustration, time
pressure, effort and mental workload, and they wrote measurably shorter replies. The paper's value
is that it separates two things usually conflated - the wall-clock cost of an interruption and its
cost to the person absorbing it - and shows that people pay the second to avoid the first.

**Key mechanism**
- 3x2 factorial design: interruption context (none / same / different) within subjects, fully
  counter-balanced; media type (telephone / instant message) between subjects
- Each condition required answering 12 emails against a supplied fact sheet, with interruptions
  delivered by an experimenter posing as the subject's supervisor every two minutes
- Task time was measured as total time minus time spent handling interruptions, so the interruption
  itself is not charged to the task; what is left is the reorientation cost
- Subjective load captured with a modified NASA Task Load Index, adding an explicit stress scale to
  the standard six
- Personality covariates measured beforehand: openness to experience (NEO-PI-R subscale) and
  personal need for structure

**Main findings**
- Interrupted tasks were completed in *less* time than the uninterrupted baseline: 22.77 min
  baseline vs 20.31 min same-context and 20.60 min different-context (p<.05 for both contrasts)
- Same-context and different-context interruptions did not differ from each other, so the contextual
  hypothesis the study was built to test was not supported
- Output shrank rather than degrading: mean email length fell from 31.49 words at baseline to 29.17
  (same context) and 30.16 (different context), p<.04
- Quality held flat - no significant difference in error counts (p<.19) or in the politeness metric
  (p<.91) across conditions
- Every subjective cost rose significantly under interruption: stress 6.92 to 9.46/9.13 (p<.001),
  frustration 4.73 to 6.63/6.48 (p<.007), time pressure 11.02 to 12.69/12.17 (p<.01), effort 9.50 to
  11.04/11.52 (p<.001), mental workload (p<.03), all on a 20-point scale
- The effect appeared after roughly 20 minutes of interrupted work, which is a short exposure for
  costs of that size
- Openness to experience and need for personal structure both predicted faster completion of
  interrupted work (R-squared .14, p<.04), so tolerance is partly dispositional
- Media type had no effect and no interaction, so the result is not an artefact of telephone versus IM

**Key takeaways**
- Do not evaluate an interruption by elapsed time; the measured penalty is in stress and effort,
  which a time-based metric reports as an improvement
- People absorb interruption cost by compressing their output - the shorter emails here are the
  visible form of that, and shorter output is not automatically cheaper output
- Matching an interruption to the current topic buys nothing, so tooling that tries to schedule
  interruptions by subject relevance is optimising a variable this experiment found inert
- The lever that does exist is the *number* of discontinuities over time, not their content
- Sample is 48 mostly German university students, mean age 26, on a simulated office task, so the
  effect sizes are laboratory estimates and should not be quoted as field magnitudes
- The design measures reorientation cost only after the interruption ends; it does not isolate the
  moment-by-moment cost immediately following one, which the authors flag as unfinished work

**Relevance**
- Grounds the argument for keeping tracking inside the working session rather than in a separate
  tool, and grounds it on the right axis: the measured penalties are mental workload, stress,
  frustration, time pressure and effort, so this is evidence about cognitive load, not about time.
  A claim that context switching "costs time" would misreport the paper, which found the opposite
- Bounds the claim honestly - this is human interruption in a lab email task, not an agent leaving a
  coding session, so it is suggestive of the mechanism rather than evidence about agents
